Master Oogway

Combat Robotics at Berkeley
Aug 2024 – Present
Project Manager

Led a team of six engineers to design and fabricate a 15lb combat robot for Sacramento Bot Battles. Delivered a competitive system within a $1,000 budget through iterative prototyping and FEA-driven optimization.

The Challenge

Design and build a competitive 15lb combat robot within a strict $1,000 budget while prioritizing durability and consistent functionality. The robot needed to deliver high KE while being efficient to manufacture and assemble, allowing for rapid repairs during competition. Traditional vertical spinner designs required expensive full-weapon CNC machining, pushing us beyond budget constraints.

Drisk Weapon Design

We chose a drisk configuration (drum + disk) to create an asymmetrical vertical spinner while significantly reducing costs. Instead of CNCing the entire steel weapon, we only machined the aluminum hub connecting the disks. This design decision reduced manufacturing costs by 31% while maintaining competitive KE. Through 10+ iterations, we refined the weapon geometry to optimize impact energy delivery while maintaining structural integrity.

The Torsional Force Problem

The drisk design created a critical engineering challenge: impacts to only one disk would create massive torsional forces on the aluminum hub. Since aluminum is softer than steel, we had serious concerns about disks shearing off the hub or twisting it during high-energy impacts. Traditional bolt patterns wouldn't provide enough resistance to these rotational forces.

I designed a keyed flower pattern that provides additional support to the hub/disk interface. The keys interlock with slots in the disks, distributing torsional loads across multiple contact points rather than relying solely on friction. I validated this solution with FEA using hand-calculated impact forces, confirming minimal deformation in the hub even under worst-case single-disk impacts.

Hub Assembly & Cross-Section

The complete weapon hub assembly shows how all components integrate—from the keyed flower interface to the bearing mounts and pulley system. The cross-sectional view reveals the internal structure, showing thrust bearings, needle roller bearings, and the custom aluminum hub that connects the asymmetric weapon disks while handling 1.5 kJ of rotational energy.

GD&T Drawings

The weapon hub shop drawings show the GD&T specifications we sent to our sponsor to manufacture. The guiding principle behind our drawings was to assign tight tolerances only to critical features. This approach simplifies manufacturing—since machinists don't have to hold unnecessary precision—and reduces cost on our end. For the hub specifically, precise tolerances weren't required because none of the interfaces relied on a press fit. A standard clearance fit was sufficient, as long as the disk-to-hub interface maintained adequate clearance and the key provided the torsional resistance. The drawing below shows the shop drawing I created so that we could manually drill press and tap the holes needed for the screws.

Mitigating Gyroscopic Effect

Vertical spinners at high RPM create a gyroscopic effect that makes robots difficult to control—the spinning mass resists changes in orientation, causing the robot to tip or become unresponsive to steering inputs. This problem is essentially unavoidable, but I optimized our design to minimize it. I selected a 1.3:1 pulley ratio for 9,000 RPM max speed with 1.5 kJ of kinetic energy, and widened the drive base for improved stability. These optimizations reduced the adverse gyroscopic effect by 21% while maintaining competitive striking power.

Complete Robot Assembly

The final cutaway view shows the complete Master Oogway assembly—4WD brushless drivetrain, weapon system with pulley reduction, electronics integration, and structural chassis. Every component was designed to work together as a cohesive system, balancing offensive capability with defensive durability within our tight budget constraints.

Self-Driving Car Simulation

Personal Project
2024 – Present

Developed an autonomous vehicle simulator from scratch using JavaScript and HTML Canvas. Built a fully interpretable self-driving AI without external libraries, focusing on real-time simulation, neuroevolution, and neural network visualization.

The Challenge

Most self-driving car implementations rely on black-box libraries and pre-trained models, making it difficult to understand how the AI makes decisions. I wanted to build a fully interpretable autonomous driving system from the ground up—no TensorFlow, no external ML libraries—just pure JavaScript. The goal was to create a system where every neuron activation, every weight, and every decision could be visualized and understood in real-time.

Custom Neural Network

Designed a feedforward neural network from scratch with a 5-6-4 topology (5 sensor inputs, 6 hidden neurons, 4 control outputs). Every weight and bias is visualized in real-time, enabling direct inspection of AI decision pathways. The network processes sensor data and outputs steering, throttle, reverse, and brake commands. This transparency was key to understanding and debugging autonomous driving behavior without relying on opaque library functions.

LIDAR-Inspired Sensor System

Implemented a raycasting sensor array to simulate how real autonomous cars perceive their surroundings. Each ray calculates distance to obstacles using polygon intersection algorithms and feeds normalized values (0-1) into the neural network. This design provided deterministic, interpretable perception outputs with tunable ray count and detection ranges, allowing the AI to "see" walls and traffic in real-time.

Custom Physics Engine

Developed a lightweight physics system from scratch to handle realistic vehicle dynamics—steering angles, acceleration/braking forces, friction modeling, and polygon-based collision detection. Combined with the neural network, this enabled smooth, realistic vehicle control without complex algorithms. The physics engine runs at 60 FPS while simultaneously training multiple AI agents.

Training Without Supervision

Instead of manually tuning thousands of weights, I implemented a genetic algorithm for autonomous training. The best-performing car is saved as the "elite" genome, and new generations are created through random weight mutations. Fitness is defined by distance traveled without collision. This neuroevolution approach allowed the network to rapidly improve driving ability across hundreds of generations without any supervised learning or labeled training data.

In the demo on the right you can see the Neuroevolution happening. The sensor rays are projected from the current best car in the simulation: define as the one with the greatest y-value. Every other car is slightly translucent, and the cars that have failed have been grayed out to make it easier to visualize the failed mutations.

Differential Mount & Chain Tensioner

UC Berkeley
Jan 2025 – Feb 2025

Designed a complete differential mounting system for a chain-driven drivetrain. Integrated a turnbuckle-based chain tensioning system and optimized all components through FEA to achieve a 1.5 factor of safety.

The Challenge

Design a differential mounting system that securely attaches to the rear chassis while meeting strict competition rules. The mount needed to integrate all required hardware—bearings, fasteners, tabs, and a jacking bar—while incorporating a chain-tensioning system. Chain-driven systems require constant tension to prevent skipping, but the mounting geometry made traditional tensioning methods impossible. The entire system had to be lightweight yet strong enough to handle motor torque and shock loads during competition.

Selecting the Tensioning Method & Force Hand Calculations

I compared three chain-tensioning approaches: idler sprockets (add weight and complexity), eccentric tensioners (difficult to adjust precisely), and turnbuckles (lightweight and adjustable). I chose a turnbuckle because it's lightweight, durable, and allows precise, dynamic adjustment of chain tension. However, this decision created a new challenge—the differential mount design needed to integrate around the turnbuckle's specific loading requirements.

The Tension vs. Compression Problem

Turnbuckles only work properly when loaded in tension—they'll buckle and fail if loaded in compression. In our initial layout, mounting the turnbuckle directly to the differential would have put it in compression as the chain pulls the differential forward. This was a fundamental mechanical problem: the geometry required for chain tension was incompatible with the turnbuckle's load requirements. I solved this by designing the differential mount to pivot on a top tab and sit slightly behind the chassis mounting point. This clever geometry reversal allowed the turnbuckle to mount to the chassis while pulling the differential mount backward—keeping the chain tight and ensuring the turnbuckle stays in tension. The pivot point became the fulcrum that converted the chain's forward pull into a backward tension force on the turnbuckle.

FEA-Driven Optimization

Performed detailed hand calculations to estimate forces on the mount: chain wrap angle, motor-induced forces, resulting moments on each side of the differential, and expected shock loads from impacts and acceleration. Used these forces in SolidWorks FEA to iteratively add or remove material until achieving a factor of safety of 1.5 while minimizing weight. The final mounts weighed just 115g and 150g—50% lighter than the original jacking bar design while maintaining structural integrity across all load cases.
