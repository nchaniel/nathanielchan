2nd-Gen Electronic Adjustable Spacer Pipette

A spacer pipette fans the nozzles of a multichannel pipette in and out so a scientist can move liquid between vessel formats. I took Rainin's manual mechanism and rebuilt the liquid end as an electronic, moldable, leak-tight assembly for their Vero handle.

The brief

Rainin's current adjustable spacer pipette is manual — the user twists a dial to change nozzle spacing. The goal was a second-generation electronic liquid end compatible with the new Vero handle, and I was asked to improve three things: the leaks appearing in assembled units, the size and weight of the liquid end, and the spacing range — extending it to 384-tip (4.5 mm) formats.

What I changed

I replaced the secondary constraint — a flat beam — with a cylindrical bore in a single-piece slider. It's far simpler to machine precisely, it kills nozzle wobble and barrel-cam binding, and one part now serves both LTS and universal tips. Rearranging and re-molding the cylinders collapsed the baseplate geometry that was driving both part count and leak paths.

The electronics

I drove the barrel cam with an N20 DC gearmotor (298:1) through an H-bridge, with a magnetic encoder for position feedback, and wrote the Arduino control firmware: a four-stage homing and stall-detection state machine plus position and freedrive modes. That took the mechanism from concept to a working MVP.

The archetype

Rather than machine an entire steel weapon — the expensive path — we chose a drisk (drum + disk) archetype: a custom aluminum hub carrying asymmetric steel disks. It cuts both cost and lead time, and it gave us an asymmetric vertical spinner that still hits hard. We iterated through 10+ weapon prototypes, always prioritizing durability and how fast we could repair the bot between fights.

Driving cost down through the disk

Ten-plus iterations on the disk and hub geometry took machining cost down 31%. Only the aluminum hub is machined; the asymmetric disks are waterjet and laser-cut steel. The shop drawings that made those parts were mine to produce.

A keyed "flower" hub

A single-disk impact dumps a large torsional shock into a soft aluminum hub. I designed a six-petal keyed interface that spreads that shock across all six contact points instead of relying on the mounting screws to take it in shear — validated in FEA against hand-calculated worst-case impact loads.

Spinning it up — and keeping it drivable

I sized the motor and belt reduction to reach 9,000 RPM and 1.5 kJ, then designed a bent motor mount that removes the cantilevered overhang on the brushless motor. Tuning the operating RPM cut gyroscopic drift 21% without giving up striking power.

20:1 Cycloidal Gearbox

The AI Rover arm previously used store-bought planetary gearboxes with a pulley drive and a chain tensioner. A 10 kg payload needs a high reduction, so I designed a cycloidal gearbox instead — high ratio in a small package, cheaper than buying planetary, and far friendlier to 3D printing.

RoleGearbox design · part selection JointShoulder — peak arm torque
Sizing: a 10 kg load at worst-case full extension, plus estimated linkage and joint weight. Against the selected motor's stall torque with a 1.5× safety factor, that's a 200:1 total reduction — which leaves the cycloidal stage at 20:1 after a 10:1 compound gear train.

Why cycloidal

Fine-tooth printed gears build friction and heat and are prone to binding. Cycloidal disks aren't — they're more robust, have less backlash, and hold up better in service. A cycloidal drive also reaches a high reduction in a small form factor, and it's cheaper to make than a bought planetary unit.

Printed to the core

Every principal part is FDM-printable — no machined internals. I calculated the eccentricity values and built the eccentric cam from 3D-printed parts mounted with screws. Nylon spacers replace ball bearings, taking bearing cost to near-zero on a student budget, and the gearbox stays back-drivable for easy bench testing.

Also — earlier arm revision

2-DOF Wrist · Gripper Mount

An earlier gripper mount, designed around a long lead screw that actuates the jaw. Because the screw has to extend well behind the jaw, the gripper couldn't sit straight off the arm's final linkage.

I offset the mount from the last linkage and used a two-gear pair to drive wrist roll around that offset, then added a linkage for pitch — a compact 2-DOF wrist that keeps the lead screw clear.

Differential Mount & Chain Tensioner

One bracket that mounts the differential and tensions the chain. The differential sits at the rear of the car; a chain drives the rear wheels from one side, offset from the differential's centerline — so the mount has to carry a large bending moment while keeping the support bearing concentric.

RoleBracket design · analysis MethodFree-body → hand calcs → FEA

The mount is the tensioner

Chain drives need constant tension or they skip. Instead of adding a separate idler, the whole bracket pivots on a chassis tube and a turnbuckle sets the pivot angle — so all the slack is taken up with a single adjustment. I aligned the turnbuckle so it acts in tension during normal running.

The bracket is triangulated to carry the offset chain load into two frame tubes while keeping the large support bearing concentric.

From the free-body diagram: 230 N·m of motor torque over a 30 mm sprocket radius ≈ 7.7 kN of chain tension. Resolved through the 27° wrap angle, that's 6.8 kN and 3.5 kN reactions at the differential's support bearings.
Optimization

I ran the resolved loads through SolidWorks FEA and iterated the bracket geometry with topology optimization — adding and removing material until it held a minimum factor of safety of 1.72 at roughly 430 g.

Self-Driving Car Simulation

A fully interpretable autonomous-driving system built from scratch in pure JavaScript — no TensorFlow, no external ML libraries. Every neuron activation, every weight, and every decision is rendered live, so the whole control loop can be watched and understood.

StackPure JavaScript · HTML Canvas TrainingGenetic algorithm

Watching it drive

A LIDAR-inspired ray array detects walls and traffic through polygon intersection, feeding normalized 0–1 distances into the network. Everything runs at a steady 60 FPS while a full population of agents trains at once.

Interpretable by design

A feedforward network — 5 sensor inputs, 6 hidden neurons, 4 control outputs (steer, throttle, reverse, brake) — with every weight and bias drawn on screen and no ML framework behind it. Most implementations lean on black-box models; here, the behavior can be debugged by watching it.

Learning by mutation

Training is unsupervised: the elite genome is saved and the next generation mutates from it, with fitness measured as distance travelled without a collision. Rays project from the current best car; failed mutations fade to grey to keep the search legible.
